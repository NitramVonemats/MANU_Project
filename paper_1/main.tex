\begin{abstract}
Accurate prediction of molecular ADMET (Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, Excretion, and Toxicity) properties is essential for early-stage drug discovery, yet reported model performance depends heavily on evaluation protocol and hyperparameter tuning. We present a reproducible benchmarking framework that systematically evaluates seven hyperparameter optimization (HPO) strategies, like Particle Swarm Optimization, Simulated Annealing, Genetic Algorithm, Artificial Bee Colony, Hill Climbing, Random Search, and Tree-structured Parzen Estimator for Graph Neural Networks across six Therapeutics Data Commons (TDC) ADMET tasks under scaffold-split evaluation. Over 2{,}100 model training runs (50 trials $\times$ 7 algorithms $\times$ 6 datasets) reveal that no single optimizer universally dominates: Random Search wins on three of four regression tasks, while metaheuristics (SA, ABC) excel on toxicity classification. We further compare optimized GNNs against foundation model baselines (ChemBERTa, MolCLR, Morgan fingerprints) and find that task-specific GNNs with systematic HPO can match or exceed frozen pretrained encoders. Multi-seed validation and statistical significance testing confirm result robustness. Our findings demonstrate that scaffold-split evaluation substantially alters optimizer rankings compared to random-split settings, and we provide practical recommendations for HPO strategy selection under limited compute budgets.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

AI and machine learning continue to find practical applications in advancing drug discovery, with many models emerging in the literature. The bulk of this research is concerned with predicting how different molecules impact the human body at large, including but not limited to, identifying disease targets, interactions between molecules, and pharmacological properties of molecules such as absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion and toxicity (ADMET), which require lots of experimentation to measure in the lab. Thus by building sufficiently precise models for these tasks one can greatly hasten the speed at which new medicines could be researched \cite{zhang2025admet}.

To establish a comprehensive evaluation, we compare widely adopted baseline models against specialized Graph Neural Networks (GNNs). Classical algorithms like Random Forest and XGBoost utilizing pre-computed chemical descriptors, such as Morgan fingerprints, have traditionally set the standard for computational ADMET profiling due to their efficiency and robustness~\cite{wu2018moleculenet}. However, these fixed vector representations often fail to capture complex topological motifs present in three-dimensional molecular structures. GNNs address this limitation by relying on a message-passing paradigm that naturally aligns with the graph-like structure of molecules, seamlessly treating atoms as nodes and chemical bonds as edges~\cite{gilmer2017neural}. By operating directly on the raw molecular graph, GNNs learn task-specific, end-to-end representations that effectively capture rich local and long-range structural interactions. Recent literature highlights that advanced GNN architectures, such as directed message passing neural networks and attention-based spatial graph models, consistently demonstrate superior predictive performance across diverse ADMET benchmarks relative to traditional descriptor-centric baselines~\cite{yang2019analyzing, xiong2020pushing}. Consequently, evaluating these specialized GNNs provides critical insight into their capacity to model the subtle structure-activity relationships inherently required for accurate pharmacokinetic and toxicity predictions.

However, despite rapid progress in molecular machine learning, reported performance often depends heavily on evaluation protocol. In particular, scaffold-based splitting (Bemis–Murcko scaffolds \cite{doi:10.1021/jm9602928}) creates a more realistic generalization setting by separating compounds with different core structures, but it also induces a strong distribution shift. As a result, models that perform well under random splits can degrade substantially under scaffold splits, especially for chemically diverse ADMET endpoints \cite{chemberta2, yang2019analyzing}. This motivates systematic, reproducible benchmarking under scaffold-split evaluation to avoid overly optimistic conclusions.

To build models for ADMET prediction however, one requires a large thorough dataset not only for simply training and evaluation, but for additionally comparing many different models. One such group of datasets is the ADMET collection compiled by Therapeutics Data Commons \cite{huang2021therapeuticsdatacommonsmachine} which contains several datasets for predicting how molecules are absorbed, distributed, broken down and excreted by the body, as well as their toxicity measured on various organisms. Aside for the datasets, with libraries for easily obtaining them and their splits, TDC keeps leaderboards of the all the top models with their performances, some of which are given in the following section.

In this work we present a reproducible benchmarking framework for molecular property prediction that systematically evaluates  different ADMET models (baseline pretrained models and specialized optimized GNNs) as well as different GNN hyperparameter optimization (HPO) strategies across strategies for Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) across six Therapeutics Data Commons (TDC) ADMET tasks. Unlike prior work proposing new molecular architectures, our focus is on systematic evaluation methodology and optimization behavior under controlled experimental settings, enabling reproducible comparisons across datasets and HPO strategies.

The study includes 7 HPO algorithms and 50 trials per dataset-algorithm combination (2,100+ total model evaluations), alongside multi-seed validation to quantify statistical robustness. As baselines, we compare optimized GNNs against a diverse set of molecular representation approaches: classical fingerprint-based methods (Morgan fingerprints), learned fingerprint methods (MolE-style), and pretrained transformer-based foundation models (ChemBERTa, fine-tuned ChemBERTa, MolCLR).
This breadth of baselines allows us to situate GNN performance relative to both traditional cheminformatics pipelines and modern self-supervised pretraining approaches, while also documenting scaffold-split sensitivity across SMILES-based transformer architectures.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.} We make the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We present a \textbf{reproducible benchmarking framework} for molecular property prediction, evaluating \textbf{7 HPO algorithms} (Random, PSO, ABC, GA, SA, HC, TPE) across \textbf{6 ADMET datasets} from TDC under a consistent scaffold-split evaluation protocol, with multi-seed validation and confidence intervals to ensure result stability.
    \item We conduct a \textbf{systematic comparison} of HPO algorithms for GNN training through \textbf{2,100+ training runs}, analyzing optimization behavior, budget efficiency, and deriving practical recommendations on HPO strategy selection under limited compute budgets.
    \item We provide an \textbf{empirical comparison} between optimized task-specific GNNs and pretrained molecular foundation models, documenting scaffold-split sensitivity in SMILES transformers.
\end{itemize}

Peeking at the TDC ADMET Leaderboards, one finds a plethora of machine learning models designed specifically for regression and classification tasks on molecules. Surprisingly, more often than not it is some of the simpler designs that show great success. This includes models that are not fully based on deep learning, such as MapLight~\cite{maplight}, which utilizes random forests and SVMs in conjunction with node embeddings, and RDKit2D+MLP~\cite{rdkit2d_mlp}, which passes 200 physicochemical descriptors through a standard multi-layer perceptron. For certain datasets even a regular Random Forest or XGBoost achieves top results, prompting the development of FCA~\cite{cfa}, which takes a weighted combination of all of them.

Our main focus, however, lies with deep learning models. Perhaps the simplest of these is AttentiveFP~\cite{attentiveFP}, which utilizes a couple of GCN and GAT layers followed by a series of GRU layers. The resulting model yields top-5 results on many datasets, aided by the fact that it additionally combines edge features and node features at the very start. Other standard GNN architectures that appear prominently in molecular benchmarks include GCN~\cite{kipf2017gcn}, GIN~\cite{xu2019gin}, and Directed Message Passing Neural Networks (D-MPNN)~\cite{yang2019dmpnn}, which operates on bond-level messages rather than atom-level ones, achieving state-of-the-art results on several ADMET endpoints.

The rest of the deep learning models borrow many ideas utilized in large language models (LLMs). These include masking and contrastive learning with negative samples as seen in~\cite{attrMasking} and~\cite{contrativeLearning}. Masking refers to removing certain node or edge attributes and then learning optimal embeddings to predict these missing values. Contrastive learning on the other hand is a more general technique where molecular graphs are modified and embeddings are learned such that the modifications of the same graph have embeddings as similar as possible, while those from different molecules are more distant. MolCLR~\cite{molclr} extends this paradigm with three graph augmentation strategies (atom masking, bond deletion, and subgraph removal) and demonstrates that contrastive pretraining improves downstream ADMET prediction compared to training from scratch.

Masking and contrastive learning are in fact two common methods that go into molecular foundation models. For the most part these models work in a similar principle: first, embeddings are learned via self-supervised pretraining, then they are simultaneously trained on several different prediction tasks, and finally fine-tuned to a specific problem. Such models include DeepMol~\cite{deepMol}, MolE~\cite{molE}, and MegaMolBART~\cite{mmelon}. A more unique case is ChemBERTa~\cite{chemberta2}, which rather than directly learning on graphs utilizes a transformer architecture (RoBERTa) to learn embeddings of SMILES strings, pretrained on 77 million molecules from ZINC-15. Similarly, MolBERT~\cite{molbert} combines masked language modeling on SMILES with auxiliary physicochemical property prediction objectives during pretraining. A great downside of such foundation models, however, is their large size, which makes them both harder to train and less cost-effective to use in general. This is where MiniMol~\cite{miniMol} shines---it is yet another foundation model, utilizing the MPNN++ architecture, which has about 10M parameters yet yields top results for some of the datasets.

\paragraph{Scaffold Split Challenges.}
Recent work has highlighted that transformer-based models (ChemBERTa, MolBERT) can exhibit substantial distribution shift when evaluated on scaffold-split datasets, achieving high validation AUC but near-random test performance~\cite{yang2019dmpnn,chemberta2}. Wu et al.~\cite{wu2018moleculenet} introduced MoleculeNet as a standardized benchmark with scaffold splitting, demonstrating that this splitting strategy more faithfully reflects the generalization challenge in real drug discovery campaigns. Our experiments confirm this finding: ChemBERTa achieves a validation AUC of 0.82 on Tox21 but degrades to 0.46 on the scaffold-split test set, motivating careful evaluation under realistic splitting regimes.


\subsection{Hyperparameter Optimization for Molecular ML}

Hyperparameter optimization is a key driver of performance in deep learning models and is particularly important in molecular property prediction where datasets are small and noisy. Bergstra and Bengio~\cite{bergstra2012random} demonstrated that Random Search can outperform grid search by exploring more diverse configurations when only a subset of hyperparameters is influential, establishing it as a strong baseline. Bayesian optimization methods such as the Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE)~\cite{bergstra2011tpe}, implemented in frameworks like Optuna~\cite{akiba2019optuna}, model the relationship between hyperparameters and validation performance to improve sample efficiency. In molecular ML, where each training run can be costly, TPE-style methods are often attractive under limited trial budgets.

In contrast, population-based metaheuristics, like Particle Swarm Optimization~\cite{kennedy1995pso}, Genetic Algorithms~\cite{holland1975ga}, and Artificial Bee Colony~\cite{karaboga2005abc}, provide broader exploration of the search space but may require larger budgets to converge reliably. Simulated Annealing~\cite{kirkpatrick1983sa} offers a middle ground by accepting suboptimal moves early in the search, enabling escape from local optima. Despite their widespread use in combinatorial optimization, systematic comparisons of these metaheuristic HPO strategies on molecular property prediction tasks remain scarce, particularly under scaffold-split evaluation protocols. Our work addresses this gap by benchmarking all seven strategies under identical experimental conditions.


\subsection{Challenges in Molecular ML: Distribution Shifts and Generalization}
While pretrained representations can transfer across tasks, their effectiveness heavily depends on the evaluation protocol. Recent work has highlighted that foundation models, particularly transformer-based models like ChemBERTa and MolBERT, can exhibit substantial distribution shift when evaluated on scaffold-split datasets. In such scenarios, they often achieve high validation AUC but near-random test performance \cite{chemberta2, yang2019analyzing}.

Scaffold-split evaluation can expose distribution shift that leads to strong validation performance but poor test generalization for models that overfit chemical substructures or tokenization patterns. MoleculeNet \cite{wu2018moleculenet} was introduced as a standardized benchmark with scaffold splitting, demonstrating that this splitting strategy more faithfully reflects the generalization challenge in real drug discovery campaigns by partitioning molecules based on their core structural frameworks (scaffolds) rather than random selection.

These marked generalization gaps reported in the literature motivate comparing task-specific end-to-end GNN training with pretrained encoders used as frozen feature extractors and with limited fine-tuning.


\section{Materials and methods}

\subsection{Datasets and Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:datasets}

We evaluate all models on six molecular property prediction tasks drawn from the
Therapeutics Data Commons (TDC) ADMET benchmark~\cite{huang2021therapeuticsdatacommonsmachine},
covering four pharmacokinetic (ADME) regression tasks and two toxicity classification
tasks.  Figure~\ref{fig:class-distribution} provides an overview of data provenance
and class composition across all six datasets, highlighting the severe class
imbalance present in the toxicity benchmarks.

\subsubsection*{ADME Regression Tasks}

\paragraph{Caco-2 Permeability (Caco2\_Wang).}
The Caco-2 cell monolayer assay is the gold-standard \emph{in vitro} surrogate for
human intestinal absorption~\cite{artursson1991caco2}.  High apparent permeability
($P_{app} > 10^{-6}$ cm/s) is associated with good oral bioavailability.  The
dataset, curated by Wang et al.~\cite{wang2016admet}, contains \textbf{910 compounds}
with target values already expressed in log(cm/s) units; no further log transformation
is applied.  Caco-2 is considered the most tractable regression task in our benchmark
because permeability is governed primarily by molecular size, lipophilicity, and
hydrogen-bond capacity---features that GNNs can capture directly from atom graphs.

\paragraph{Half-Life Prediction (Half\_Life\_Obach).}
Plasma half-life ($t_{1/2}$, hours) determines dosing frequency and is essential for
designing drugs with adequate therapeutic windows.  However, half-life is a composite
pharmacokinetic parameter influenced not only by molecular structure but also by
volume of distribution, protein binding fraction, and individual patient
variability~\cite{obach1999prediction}.  The dataset is drawn from the Obach
FDA-approved drug collection and contains \textbf{667 compounds}
spanning a wide dynamic range (0.5--100$+$ hours).  We apply a log transformation
before standardisation to reduce target skewness.

\paragraph{Hepatocyte Clearance (Clearance\_Hepatocyte\_AZ).}
Intrinsic clearance measured in freshly isolated human hepatocytes
($\mu$L/min/10$^6$ cells) captures both phase-I and phase-II metabolic routes and
is the most physiologically relevant \emph{in vitro} metabolic stability
assay~\cite{riley2001pharma}.  The dataset was generated internally at AstraZeneca
and is distributed through TDC~\cite{huang2021therapeuticsdatacommonsmachine},
comprising \textbf{1\,213 proprietary compounds}.
Low clearance values indicate slow hepatic elimination and a longer \emph{in vivo}
half-life.  This task proved the most challenging in our benchmark, with GNN models
yielding negative R$^2$ values, indicating that molecular structure alone is
insufficient to explain the variance in hepatic metabolism (see
Section~\ref{results}).

\paragraph{Microsomal Clearance (Clearance\_Microsome\_AZ).}
Human liver microsomal assays ($\mu$L/min/mg protein) measure primarily cytochrome
P450-mediated (CYP) oxidative metabolism; they offer higher throughput than
hepatocyte assays but capture only phase-I reactions~\cite{obach2006trend}.  The
AstraZeneca dataset contains \textbf{1\,102 compounds}.
Like hepatocyte clearance, microsomal clearance values are log-transformed and
then standardised using train-set statistics.

\subsubsection*{Toxicity Classification Tasks}

\paragraph{Tox21 Nuclear Receptor -- Androgen Receptor (Tox21 NR-AR).}
The Tox21 initiative~\cite{tox21data} assembled a large multi-assay toxicology
panel spanning nuclear receptor signalling and stress response pathways to enable
high-throughput environmental hazard screening.  We use the \textbf{NR-AR} assay
(androgen receptor agonism), which identifies compounds that can disrupt androgen
signalling, a key mechanism in endocrine disruption and reproductive
toxicity.  After removing molecules with invalid SMILES, the dataset contains
\textbf{7\,258 compounds}, of which only $\approx3.5\%$ are active (positive) samples
(see Figure~\ref{fig:class-distribution})---making this the most class-imbalanced
task in our benchmark.  To mitigate the imbalance, we apply positive-class
weighting in the binary cross-entropy loss, where the weight is set to the ratio of
negative to positive samples in the training fold.

\paragraph{hERG Cardiotoxicity (hERG).}
Blockade of the hERG potassium ion channel is the predominant mechanism of
drug-induced QT interval prolongation and potentially fatal cardiac arrhythmias,
and the hERG assay is a mandatory safety screen in preclinical drug
development~\cite{sanguinetti2006herg}.  The TDC hERG dataset contains
\textbf{655 compounds} with binary blocker / non-blocker labels; approximately
31\% of compounds are blockers, making this a considerably more balanced
classification task than Tox21 NR-AR (Figure~\ref{fig:class-distribution}).

\paragraph{Splitting and evaluation protocol.}
We use the TDC 2-way scaffold split function, which partitions compounds by
Bemis--Murcko scaffolds~\cite{doi:10.1021/jm9602928} so that molecules with
structurally distinct core rings appear in different folds.  This mirrors realistic
drug-discovery campaigns, where models must generalise to novel chemical series.  The
TDC scaffold split provides train and test sets; we further subdivide the train set
90/10 (train/validation) using a fixed random seed (42) to enable early stopping and
model selection without touching the held-out test set.  The final split proportions
are approximately \textbf{80 / 10 / 10} (train / val / test) across all datasets.

During hyperparameter optimisation (HPO), the test split remains \emph{fully held
out}; model selection is performed solely on the validation metric.  Final performance
is reported once on the test split after HPO is complete, preventing any implicit
information leakage from repeated test-set evaluations.

\paragraph{Target transformations.}
For the three skewed regression tasks (Half-Life, Hepatocyte and Microsomal
Clearance) we apply $y' = \log(y + \epsilon)$, where $\epsilon = 10^{-3}$ is a
small offset to handle near-zero values, followed by z-score normalisation using
training-set statistics.  Caco-2 targets are already in log(cm/s) units, so only
z-score normalisation is applied.  All normalisation parameters are computed
exclusively on the training fold and reused for validation and test sets to prevent
data leakage.  Classification targets are kept as binary integers (0/1) without
transformation.

\subsection{Optimization Algorithms}
\subsubsection{Genetic Algorithm}
\textbf{Genetic Algorithms} (GAs) draw inspiration from natural selection and evolutionary principles, advancing a population of candidate solutions through multiple generations \cite{gaopt}. Each candidate encodes a potential hyperparameter configuration, typically represented as a chromosome or encoded string. The population evolves through biologically-inspired operations—selection, crossover, and mutation—that progressively enhance fitness as measured by model performance metrics. For hyperparameter tuning, GAs excel at navigating complex, high-dimensional search spaces by effectively balancing exploration of new regions with exploitation of promising areas. The mutation and recombination mechanisms prove particularly valuable for escaping local optima, making GAs well-suited for non-convex and discrete hyperparameter landscapes.
\subsubsection{Particle Swarm Optimization}
\textbf{Particle Swarm Optimization} abstracts the collective behavior of bird flocks and schooling fish to solve continuous optimization problems. Within a swarm, each particle represents a candidate hyperparameter configuration and iteratively updates its position by considering both its own historical best performance and the best solution discovered by the entire swarm \cite{psoopt}. This combination of individual and collective learning creates an effective balance between exploratory and exploitative search, allowing PSO to converge efficiently toward high-quality hyperparameter sets. PSO is particularly effective for continuous and real-valued hyperparameters, offering the dual advantages of straightforward implementation and minimal hyperparameter tuning requirements for the optimizer itself.
\subsubsection{Artificial Bee Colony}
\textbf{Artificial Bee Colony} (ABC) optimization mimics the sophisticated foraging behavior observed in honeybee colonies \cite{abcopt}. The algorithm maintains a colony of three bee types—employed bees, onlookers, and scouts—each contributing distinct exploration and exploitation capabilities. Within this framework, candidate solutions are treated as nectar sources, and bees collaboratively search nearby solutions, assess their quality, and disseminate information throughout the colony. The ABC algorithm dynamically equilibrates exploration and exploitation, achieving robust performance across both discrete and continuous hyperparameter spaces. Its competitive results compared to other swarm-based metaheuristics demonstrate particular strength in tackling complex, multimodal optimization landscapes.
\subsubsection{Hill Climbing}
\textbf{Hill Climbing} constitutes one of the most elementary optimization heuristics, yet provides a valuable baseline reference for hyperparameter optimization tasks. The algorithm begins with an initial hyperparameter configuration and only transitions to a neighboring configuration when it demonstrates improved model performance \cite{hcopt}. This greedy approach enables fast convergence in smooth, unimodal search landscapes. Nevertheless, the complete absence of local-optima escape mechanisms renders hill climbing vulnerable to premature convergence, particularly in the non-convex hyperparameter spaces common in contemporary deep learning applications. Despite this vulnerability, hill climbing remains practical when the hyperparameter space is limited in dimensionality or when adequate initialization strategies are available.
\subsubsection{Random Search}
\textbf{Random search} stands out as an effective baseline approach for hyperparameter optimization \cite{bergstra2012random}, operating by uniformly sampling hyperparameter configurations across the search space. This method proves particularly valuable in high-dimensional optimization problems where only a limited subset of hyperparameters meaningfully influence performance outcomes. Research has shown that random search frequently surpasses grid search when the search space contains non-critical hyperparameters, as it allocates computational resources more effectively by exploring diverse configurations instead of exhaustively examining irrelevant parameter combinations. The approach offers several practical advantages, like the fact that it is conceptually straightforward, readily parallelizable, and requires no prior assumptions about the underlying search space structure. These properties have established random search as a standard first-line methodology, commonly deployed before transitioning to more sophisticated optimization algorithms.
\subsubsection{Simulated Annealing}
\textbf{Simulated Annealing} (SA) adapts the physical annealing process used in metallurgy, heating and gradual cooling to produce stable crystal structures, to optimization problems. The algorithm systematically explores the solution space while accepting both performance improvements and strategic downhill moves, a feature that helps circumvent entrapment in local optima. When applied to hyperparameter optimization, SA initiates with an initial configuration and iteratively generates neighboring candidates through controlled perturbations \cite{saopt}. Acceptance decisions incorporate both performance gains and a temperature-dependent probability that permits uphill moves early in execution. As temperature gradually declines, the algorithm transitions toward a more conservative mode, concentrating optimization efforts around the best configurations identified throughout the search.

\subsubsection{Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (Optuna)}
Hyperparameter optimization is performed using \textbf{Optuna} with the Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) \cite{akiba2019optuna} as the selected sampling strategy. TPE is a Bayesian optimization algorithm that models the distributions of high-performing and low-performing hyperparameter configurations separately. By leveraging these probabilistic models, it proposes new candidate configurations that are more likely to yield improved objective values, enabling a focused and efficient exploration of the search space.
Optuna's define-by-run paradigm allows for the dynamic and conditional construction of search spaces, offering substantial flexibility when defining complex hyperparameter relationships. In addition, its built-in pruning mechanisms evaluate intermediate results during training and terminate underperforming trials early, thereby reducing computational overhead. Combined with TPE, these features provide an adaptive and resource-efficient approach to hyperparameter optimization.

\subsection{Foundation Model Baselines}
\label{sec:foundation_baselines}

We evaluate several representation-learning baselines to contextualize the optimized GNN performance: (i)~Morgan fingerprints (ECFP) with an MLP predictor, (ii)~ChemBERTa as a SMILES transformer encoder, and (iii)~MolCLR-style contrastive graph embeddings. We consider two settings for pretrained encoders: \textbf{frozen encoder + trainable MLP head} and \textbf{limited fine-tuning} (ChemBERTa-FT) where we fine-tune the encoder with a small learning-rate sweep while keeping the downstream head fixed.

For the frozen setting, embeddings are extracted once per molecule and an MLP is trained on the train split, with early stopping based on the validation split. For fairness under compute constraints, baselines use a fixed head architecture and a small learning-rate sweep, while the GNN receives the full 50-trial HPO budget. All baselines use the same dataset splits and evaluation metrics as the GNN experiments.

\subsection{Benchmarking Framework Overview}
The benchmarking framework represents a systematic approach to evaluating, comparing, and optimizing Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) and Foundation Models for molecular property prediction in ADME (Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, and Excretion) and Toxicity analysis. In computational drug discovery, where accurate prediction of pharmacokinetic properties determines drug candidate viability, such a framework becomes indispensable for ensuring reproducible and scientifically rigorous model evaluation. This infrastructure enables researchers to identify performance bottlenecks in neural architectures (Section~\ref{sec:models}), validate architectural modifications and hyperparameter choices, and ensure predictive models meet stringent accuracy requirements across diverse molecular datasets including half-life prediction, hepatocyte clearance, microsomal clearance, cell permeability, and critical toxicity endpoints (Section~\ref{sec:datasets}).

\subsubsection{Architectural Design and Components}
At the core of this framework lies a modular architecture ensuring flexibility and extensibility, qualities essential for machine learning research where model architectures, evaluation protocols, and performance metrics evolve continuously. The framework comprises five fundamental components, each fulfilling a distinct yet synergistic role:

\textbf{The Benchmark Manager} orchestrates the entire evaluation process, coordinating execution of multiple model architectures across 6 diverse molecular datasets (4 ADME regression tasks and 2 Toxicity classification tasks), managing experimental configurations specifying architectural choices and hyperparameters, and ensuring consistent evaluation protocols enabling fair comparisons between fundamentally different neural network designs. This centralized orchestration eliminates inconsistencies plaguing ad-hoc evaluation methodologies.

\textbf{The Metrics Collector} implements a sophisticated multi-faceted measurement system capturing performance indicators across prediction quality, computational efficiency, and training dynamics. This component employs a plugin-based architecture where specialized metric calculators operate independently, enabling seamless integration of novel evaluation criteria such as uncertainty quantification metrics, chemical space coverage assessments, or domain-specific performance measures without requiring modifications to the core infrastructure.

\textbf{The Data Recorder} embodies separation between measurement and persistence by decoupling performance metric collection from storage implementation, supporting multiple backend options including CSV/JSON files for immediate inspection, structured databases for complex querying, and cloud storage for collaborative research environments.

\textbf{The Results Analyzer} provides comprehensive statistical analysis extending beyond simple metric aggregation to include cross-validation statistics with confidence intervals, statistical significance testing through paired t-tests and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, performance stratification across molecular subpopulations, and trend analysis revealing how model performance correlates with dataset characteristics such as molecular complexity or structural diversity.

\textbf{The Report Generator} transforms numerical results and statistical analyses into scientifically rigorous, publication-ready outputs including comprehensive performance tables, architectural comparison visualizations, ablation study summaries, and methodology documentation.

\subsubsection{Integration with GNN and Foundation Model Pipelines}

The framework integrates seamlessly with critical components of both \textbf{GNN-based} and \textbf{Foundation Model-based} pipelines, each requiring specialized benchmarking approaches.

\paragraph{GNN Model Architectures.} The framework evaluates diverse graph neural network designs, including Graph Convolutional Networks (GCN), Graph Attention Networks (GAT), Graph Isomorphism Networks (GIN), and GraphSAGE architectures. It optimizes architectural hyperparameters such as layer depth, hidden channel dimensions, dropout rates, and aggregation functions while profiling molecular featurization (graph construction time from SMILES strings) and its impact on downstream performance.

\paragraph{Foundation Models.} The framework supports benchmarking of pre-trained molecular embeddings (e.g., ChemBERTa, BioMedBERT) and fingerprint-based approaches (Morgan-FP). For these models, the Foundation Model HPO pipeline optimizes projection dimension, downstream predictor architecture (MLP depth/width), and learning dynamics, freezing the pre-trained encoder to evaluate representation quality. Performance assessment focuses on embedding extraction efficiency and transfer learning effectiveness.

The training pipeline receives comprehensive monitoring covering batch processing efficiency, gradient computation performance, parameter update overhead, and optimization strategy effectiveness including learning rate schedules, weight decay regularization, and early stopping criteria. Hyperparameter optimization procedures are tracked through search space exploration coverage, convergence to optimal configurations, and statistical reliability of performance estimates derived from cross-validation.

\subsubsection{Model Architecture and Training}
\label{sec:model_arch}

\paragraph{Model Architecture.}
We use a graph neural network backbone based on stacked Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) layers with batch normalization and ReLU activation. Each molecule is represented as a molecular graph constructed from SMILES using RDKit. Atom features include atomic number, degree, formal charge, hybridization, aromaticity, ring membership, hydrogen count, and atomic mass (8 features). The GNN encoder produces node embeddings which are aggregated into a graph-level representation using concatenated global mean pooling and global max pooling. The pooled representation is passed to an MLP prediction head with dropout and batch normalization. This design provides a stable and efficient backbone for systematic benchmarking under limited data regimes.


\textbf{Training Configuration.} All models were trained using the Adam optimizer with default parameters. For training, a batch size of 32 was employed, with a maximum of 100 epochs for regression tasks and 50 epochs for classification tasks. Early stopping was applied based on validation loss with a patience of 12 epochs. Regression models were optimized using mean squared error (MSE) loss, while classification models used binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss with logits and incorporated positive class weighting to address class imbalance. Target values were standardized to have zero mean and unit variance. Evaluation was performed with a batch size of 64, and gradient clipping was applied with a maximum norm of 1.0.

\textbf{Optimization Algorithms:} We compared seven HPO strategies:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO): population = 16
    \item Simulated Annealing (SA): $T_0=50$, cooling $\alpha=0.99$
    \item Genetic Algorithm (GA): population = 16, mutation = 0.1
    \item Artificial Bee Colony (ABC): colony size = 16, limit = 50
    \item Hill Climbing (HC): greedy local search
    \item Random Search: uniform sampling
    \item Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE): Optuna with 10 startup random trials and median pruning
\end{enumerate}

We select the best configuration based on validation RMSE (regression) or validation AUC (classification) and evaluate once on the held-out test split.

\noindent\textbf{HPO settings.} We use population size 16 for PSO/ABC/GA, SA with $T_0=50$ and cooling $\alpha=0.99$, and Optuna TPE with 10 startup random trials and median pruning. All methods share the same search space (hidden dimension, number of layers, learning rate, weight decay, dropout, and MLP head dimensions) to ensure fair comparison.

\subsubsection{Multi-Seed Validation}
To quantify robustness, we retrain the selected best configuration per dataset across three seeds $[42, 43, 44]$. We report the mean and standard deviation of the test metric across seeds. This prevents conclusions based on a single favorable run and enables more reliable comparisons between HPO strategies.

\subsubsection{Statistical Significance Testing}
We compare HPO algorithms against Random Search using paired tests on the per-seed results. For each dataset, we apply the Wilcoxon signed-rank test between an algorithm and Random Search, and we additionally report effect sizes to capture practical significance beyond p-values.

\paragraph{Algorithm Performance Ranking}

Across the four ADME regression datasets, no single algorithm consistently dominated all tasks, suggesting that the optimal choice of HPO strategy is problem-dependent. PSO, ABC, and GA achieved the best performance on Half\_Life\_Obach (RMSE = 21.66) while Random Search excelled on Caco2\_Wang (RMSE = 0.0027), Clearance\_Hepatocyte\_AZ (RMSE = 68.22), and Clearance\_Microsome\_AZ (RMSE = 38.75). Notably, Random Search, despite its simplicity, achieved the best NiaPy-based performance on 3 out of 4 regression tasks, demonstrating that sophisticated optimization is not always necessary.

\noindent\textbf{Key insight: optimization behavior under scaffold split.}
A central observation from our experiments is that scaffold-based evaluation changes the relative effectiveness of optimization strategies compared to random split settings commonly reported in prior work. Under scaffold splits,
the validation landscape becomes noisier and less smooth due to structural distribution shift, reducing the advantage of adaptive metaheuristic strategies. As a result, simpler approaches such as Random Search remain highly competitive,
while population-based methods require larger budgets to consistently outperform. This suggests that previously reported optimizer superiority may partially arise
from evaluation protocol choices rather than algorithmic advantages.

\paragraph{Convergence Characteristics}

Analysis of the optimization trajectories (Figure~\ref{fig:convergence}) reveals distinct behavioral patterns among the algorithms. PSO and ABC demonstrated rapid initial convergence, typically identifying near-optimal configurations within the first 10–15 trials out of 50 total. This aggressive exploration strategy proved particularly effective for smooth optimization landscapes like Caco2 permeability. In contrast, SA exhibited more gradual convergence with consistent improvement across all trials, making it more robust for challenging tasks with many local optima, such as the clearance datasets.

GA and HC showed inconsistent performance, occasionally achieving competitive results but often converging prematurely to suboptimal configurations. GA's underperformance may be attributed to the limited population size (16 individuals) and the fixed 50-trial budget, which may still be insufficient for effective evolutionary search in a high-dimensional hyperparameter space.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This work presents a comprehensive benchmarking framework for evaluating Graph Neural Networks on molecular property prediction tasks, with systematic hyperparameter optimization across six diverse ADME and toxicity datasets. Our investigation of seven optimization algorithms—Particle Swarm Optimization, Simulated Annealing, Genetic Algorithm, Artificial Bee Colony, Hill Climbing, Random Search, and Tree-structured Parzen Estimator—reveals important insights for computational drug discovery research.


\subsection{Key Findings}

\textbf{No universal optimizer exists.} Our results demonstrate that no single hyperparameter optimization algorithm dominates across all molecular property prediction tasks. PSO and Random Search achieved the best balance between performance and computational efficiency, with PSO excelling on pharmacokinetic tasks (Half-Life), while Random Search won on three regression datasets (Caco2, Clearance\_Hepatocyte, and Clearance\_Microsome). TPE excelled on the complex Clearance\_Hepatocyte task, and metaheuristic algorithms (SA, ABC) won on the toxicity classification tasks. This task-dependent performance suggests that practitioners should evaluate multiple optimization strategies rather than relying on a single approach.

\textbf{Dataset difficulty varies dramatically.} The benchmarked tasks exhibited vastly different levels of predictability from molecular structure alone. Caco-2 permeability achieved moderate performance (R² = 0.482), demonstrating that in vitro transport can be partially predicted from graph structure. In contrast, half-life prediction (R² = 0.004) and hepatocyte clearance (R² = -1.019) proved nearly impossible, highlighting fundamental limitations of structure-only models for complex pharmacokinetic processes influenced by enzyme kinetics, protein binding, and individual patient variation.

\textbf{Toxicity classification shows promise.} Both toxicity endpoints achieved strong discriminative performance (hERG AUC = 0.825, Tox21 AUC = 0.742), indicating that GNNs can effectively learn structural alerts for safety liabilities. The high F1 score on hERG (0.809) demonstrates practical utility for virtual screening to identify cardiotoxic compounds early in drug development, potentially reducing late-stage attrition.

\textbf{Simple methods remain competitive.} Random Search, despite being the simplest baseline, achieved best or near-best performance on half of the datasets. No metaheuristic algorithm achieved a statistically significant improvement over Random Search across all tasks. This finding challenges the assumption that sophisticated metaheuristic optimization is always necessary. For smooth hyperparameter landscapes with a 50-trial optimization budget, random exploration can be as effective as adaptive strategies. However, metaheuristics like PSO and SA demonstrated more consistent performance across diverse tasks.

\textbf{Foundation models require careful tuning.} Our comparison between optimized GNNs and frozen foundation models (ChemBERTa, Morgan fingerprints) revealed that task-specific GNN architectures with systematic HPO can match or exceed pretrained models used as fixed feature extractors. However, the comparison reflects a practical resource-constrained setting: the GNN is optimized with a full HPO budget, while pretrained baselines are evaluated with a limited tuning protocol commonly used in practice.


\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}

\textbf{Limited HPO budget.} Our study uses a 50-trial budget per algorithm, though larger budgets may further improve population-based methods.


\textbf{Frozen foundation models.} Our comparison used ChemBERTa as a frozen
encoder rather than fine-tuning transformer weights, which sacrifices
substantial performance. However, even with equal HPO budgets, ChemBERTa
exhibited severe scaffold split sensitivity (Tox21: Val AUC=0.82, Test AUC=0.46), suggesting fundamental limitations beyond hyperparameter choice.

While we report multi-seed retraining, future work should evaluate multi-seed HPO runs to quantify optimizer stability.


\textbf{Class imbalance strategies.} The Tox21 dataset suffered from extreme class imbalance (3.5\% positive), resulting in poor minority class recall despite high accuracy. Future studies should explore resampling techniques (SMOTE for graphs), class-weighted loss functions, and focal loss to improve sensitivity on rare toxic compounds.

\textbf{Molecular featurization alternatives.} This study used standard atom and bond features (atomic number, chirality, bond type, aromaticity). Incorporating 3D conformer information, learned atomic embeddings, or quantum chemical descriptors may improve performance on physicochemical tasks like permeability and clearance.
