\begin{abstract}
Accurate prediction of molecular ADMET (Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, Excretion, and Toxicity) properties is essential for early-stage drug discovery, yet reported model performance depends heavily on evaluation protocol and hyperparameter tuning. We present a reproducible benchmarking framework that systematically evaluates seven hyperparameter optimization (HPO) strategies---namely Particle Swarm Optimization, Simulated Annealing, Genetic Algorithm, Artificial Bee Colony, Hill Climbing, Random Search, and Tree-structured Parzen Estimator for Graph Neural Networks across six Therapeutics Data Commons (TDC) ADMET tasks under scaffold-split evaluation. Over 2{,}100 model training runs (50 trials $\times$ 7 algorithms $\times$ 6 datasets) reveal that no single optimizer universally dominates: Random Search wins on three of four regression tasks, while metaheuristics (SA, ABC) excel on toxicity classification. We further compare optimized GNNs against foundation model baselines (ChemBERTa, MolCLR, Morgan fingerprints) and find that task-specific GNNs with systematic HPO can match or exceed frozen pretrained encoders on most tasks, though pretrained representations prove more robust on the most challenging metabolic endpoint. Multi-seed validation and statistical significance testing confirm result robustness. Our findings demonstrate that scaffold-split evaluation substantially alters optimizer rankings compared to random-split settings, and we provide practical recommendations for HPO strategy selection under limited compute budgets.
\end{abstract}

\paragraph*{Keywords:} graph neural networks, hyperparameter optimization, ADMET prediction, molecular property prediction, scaffold split, drug discovery, metaheuristic optimization

\section{Introduction}

Machine learning has become an increasingly powerful tool in drug discovery, with a growing number of models targeting molecular property prediction. A central application is the prediction of pharmacological properties---absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET)---which traditionally require extensive and costly experimental characterization. Accurate computational models for these endpoints can substantially accelerate the drug development pipeline by enabling early identification of promising candidates and flagging potential liabilities before synthesis~\cite{zhang2025admet}.

To establish a comprehensive evaluation, we compare widely adopted baseline models against specialized Graph Neural Networks (GNNs). Classical algorithms like Random Forest and XGBoost utilizing pre-computed chemical descriptors, such as Morgan fingerprints, have traditionally set the standard for computational ADMET profiling due to their efficiency and robustness~\cite{wu2018moleculenet}. However, these fixed vector representations often fail to capture complex topological motifs present in three-dimensional molecular structures. GNNs address this limitation by relying on a message-passing paradigm that naturally aligns with the graph-like structure of molecules, seamlessly treating atoms as nodes and chemical bonds as edges~\cite{gilmer2017neural}. By operating directly on the raw molecular graph, GNNs learn task-specific, end-to-end representations that effectively capture rich local and long-range structural interactions. Recent literature highlights that advanced GNN architectures, such as directed message passing neural networks and attention-based spatial graph models, consistently demonstrate superior predictive performance across diverse ADMET benchmarks relative to traditional descriptor-centric baselines~\cite{yang2019analyzing, xiong2020pushing}. Consequently, evaluating these specialized GNNs provides critical insight into their capacity to model the subtle structure-activity relationships inherently required for accurate pharmacokinetic and toxicity predictions.

However, despite rapid progress in molecular machine learning, reported performance often depends heavily on evaluation protocol. In particular, scaffold-based splitting (Bemis–Murcko scaffolds \cite{doi:10.1021/jm9602928}) creates a more realistic generalization setting by separating compounds with different core structures, but it also induces a strong distribution shift. As a result, models that perform well under random splits can degrade substantially under scaffold splits, especially for chemically diverse ADMET endpoints \cite{chemberta2, yang2019analyzing}. This motivates systematic, reproducible benchmarking under scaffold-split evaluation to avoid overly optimistic conclusions.

Systematic benchmarking of ADMET models requires standardised datasets with consistent splitting protocols to ensure fair comparison. The Therapeutics Data Commons (TDC)~\cite{huang2021therapeuticsdatacommonsmachine} provides such a resource: a curated collection of ADMET datasets spanning absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity endpoints, together with standardised scaffold-based data splits and public leaderboards tracking state-of-the-art performance. We draw our evaluation tasks from the TDC ADMET benchmark; representative leaderboard entries are reviewed in the following section.

In this work we present a reproducible benchmarking framework for molecular property prediction that systematically evaluates different ADMET models---baseline pretrained models and specialized optimized GNNs---as well as seven hyperparameter optimization (HPO) strategies across six Therapeutics Data Commons (TDC) ADMET tasks. Unlike prior work proposing new molecular architectures, our focus is on systematic evaluation methodology and optimization behavior under controlled experimental settings, enabling reproducible comparisons across datasets and HPO strategies.

The study includes 7 HPO algorithms and 50 trials per dataset-algorithm combination (2,100+ total model evaluations), alongside multi-seed validation to quantify statistical robustness. As baselines, we compare optimized GNNs against a diverse set of molecular representation approaches: classical fingerprint-based methods (Morgan fingerprints), learned fingerprint methods (MolE-style), and pretrained transformer-based foundation models (ChemBERTa, fine-tuned ChemBERTa, MolCLR).
This breadth of baselines allows us to situate GNN performance relative to both traditional cheminformatics pipelines and modern self-supervised pretraining approaches, while also documenting scaffold-split sensitivity across SMILES-based transformer architectures.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.} We make the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We present a \textbf{reproducible benchmarking framework} for molecular property prediction, evaluating \textbf{7 HPO algorithms} (Random, PSO, ABC, GA, SA, HC, TPE) across \textbf{6 ADMET datasets} from TDC under a consistent scaffold-split evaluation protocol, with multi-seed validation and confidence intervals to ensure result stability.
    \item We conduct a \textbf{systematic comparison} of HPO algorithms for GNN training through \textbf{2,100+ training runs}, analyzing optimization behavior, budget efficiency, and deriving practical recommendations on HPO strategy selection under limited compute budgets.
    \item We provide an \textbf{empirical comparison} between optimized task-specific GNNs and pretrained molecular foundation models, documenting scaffold-split sensitivity in SMILES transformers.
\end{itemize}

The TDC ADMET Leaderboards reveal a diverse landscape of machine learning models for molecular regression and classification tasks. Notably, several of the top-performing approaches rely on comparatively simple architectures. These include models that are not fully based on deep learning, such as MapLight~\cite{maplight}, which utilizes random forests and SVMs in conjunction with node embeddings, and RDKit2D+MLP~\cite{rdkit2d_mlp}, which passes 200 physicochemical descriptors through a standard multi-layer perceptron. For certain datasets even a regular Random Forest or XGBoost achieves top results, motivating ensemble approaches such as FCA~\cite{cfa}, which takes a weighted combination of multiple classifiers.

Our primary focus, however, is on deep learning approaches. Among GNN-based methods, AttentiveFP~\cite{attentiveFP} employs several GCN and GAT layers followed by GRU-based attention readout, achieving top-5 results on many TDC datasets by incorporating both edge and node features from the outset. Other standard GNN architectures that appear prominently in molecular benchmarks include GCN~\cite{kipf2017gcn}, GIN~\cite{xu2019gin}, and Directed Message Passing Neural Networks (D-MPNN)~\cite{yang2019dmpnn}, which operate on bond-level messages rather than atom-level ones, achieving state-of-the-art results on several ADMET endpoints.

Beyond supervised GNN training, self-supervised pretraining strategies adapted from natural language processing have been applied to molecular graphs. Two prominent paradigms are attribute masking and contrastive learning~\cite{attrMasking, contrativeLearning}. Attribute masking removes node or edge features and trains the encoder to reconstruct them, encouraging the model to learn chemically meaningful representations. Contrastive learning instead generates augmented views of molecular graphs and trains the encoder to produce similar embeddings for augmentations of the same molecule while pushing apart embeddings from different molecules. MolCLR~\cite{molclr} extends this paradigm with three graph augmentation strategies (atom masking, bond deletion, and subgraph removal) and demonstrates that contrastive pretraining improves downstream ADMET prediction compared to training from scratch.

Masking and contrastive learning form the basis of most molecular foundation models, which follow a common training paradigm: self-supervised pretraining on large unlabelled corpora, optional multi-task supervised training, and finally task-specific fine-tuning. Representative models in this category include DeepMol~\cite{deepMol}, MolE~\cite{molE}, and MegaMolBART~\cite{mmelon}. A distinct approach is taken by ChemBERTa~\cite{chemberta2}, which applies a transformer architecture (RoBERTa) to SMILES strings rather than molecular graphs, pretrained on 77 million molecules from ZINC-15. Similarly, MolBERT~\cite{molbert} combines masked language modeling on SMILES with auxiliary physicochemical property prediction objectives during pretraining. A notable limitation of such foundation models is their large parameter count, which increases both training cost and inference latency. MiniMol~\cite{miniMol} addresses this concern by employing the MPNN++ architecture with approximately 10M parameters, achieving competitive performance on several TDC datasets while maintaining a substantially smaller computational footprint.

\paragraph{Scaffold Split Challenges.}
Recent work has highlighted that transformer-based models (ChemBERTa, MolBERT) can exhibit substantial distribution shift when evaluated on scaffold-split datasets, achieving high validation AUC but near-random test performance~\cite{yang2019dmpnn,chemberta2}. Wu et al.~\cite{wu2018moleculenet} introduced MoleculeNet as a standardized benchmark with scaffold splitting, demonstrating that this splitting strategy more faithfully reflects the generalization challenge in real drug discovery campaigns. Our experiments confirm this finding: on the Tox21 NR-AR task, ChemBERTa achieves a validation AUC of 0.896 but drops to 0.73 on the scaffold-split test set, while MolCLR degrades further to a test AUC of 0.54 (near random), motivating careful evaluation under realistic splitting regimes.


\subsection{Hyperparameter Optimization for Molecular ML}

Hyperparameter optimization is a key driver of performance in deep learning models and is particularly important in molecular property prediction where datasets are small and noisy. Bergstra and Bengio~\cite{bergstra2012random} demonstrated that Random Search can outperform grid search by exploring more diverse configurations when only a subset of hyperparameters is influential, establishing it as a strong baseline. Bayesian optimization methods such as the Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE)~\cite{bergstra2011tpe}, implemented in frameworks like Optuna~\cite{akiba2019optuna}, model the relationship between hyperparameters and validation performance to improve sample efficiency. In molecular ML, where each training run can be costly, TPE-style methods are often attractive under limited trial budgets.

In contrast, population-based metaheuristics, like Particle Swarm Optimization~\cite{kennedy1995pso}, Genetic Algorithms~\cite{holland1975ga}, and Artificial Bee Colony~\cite{karaboga2005abc}, provide broader exploration of the search space but may require larger budgets to converge reliably. Simulated Annealing~\cite{saopt} offers a middle ground by accepting suboptimal moves early in the search, enabling escape from local optima. Despite their widespread use in combinatorial optimization, systematic comparisons of these metaheuristic HPO strategies on molecular property prediction tasks remain scarce, particularly under scaffold-split evaluation protocols. Our work addresses this gap by benchmarking all seven strategies under identical experimental conditions.


\subsection{Challenges in Molecular ML: Distribution Shifts and Generalization}
Beyond the scaffold-split sensitivity discussed above, molecular property prediction faces additional generalization challenges that compound evaluation difficulty. First, ADMET datasets are typically small (hundreds to low thousands of compounds), which amplifies variance across data splits and makes HPO-selected configurations sensitive to the specific training fold~\cite{wu2018moleculenet}. Second, the relationship between molecular structure and pharmacokinetic endpoints is often indirect: properties such as clearance and half-life depend on enzyme kinetics, transporter activity, and protein binding---factors not explicitly encoded in molecular graphs or SMILES strings~\cite{obach1999prediction}. Third, class imbalance in toxicity assays (e.g., 3.5\% positive rate in Tox21 NR-AR) biases standard loss functions toward the majority class, necessitating task-specific mitigation strategies such as positive-class weighting.

These compounding factors mean that reported model rankings can shift substantially depending on dataset size, splitting protocol, and evaluation metric. Consequently, robust benchmarking requires multi-seed validation, statistical significance testing, and explicit reporting of confidence intervals---practices that remain inconsistent in the molecular ML literature. Our framework addresses these gaps by enforcing scaffold-split evaluation, multi-seed retraining, and paired statistical tests across all comparisons.


\section{Materials and methods}

\subsection{Datasets and Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:datasets}

We evaluate all models on six molecular property prediction tasks drawn from the
Therapeutics Data Commons (TDC) ADMET benchmark~\cite{huang2021therapeuticsdatacommonsmachine},
covering four pharmacokinetic (ADME) regression tasks and two toxicity classification
tasks.  Figure~\ref{fig:class-distribution} provides an overview of data provenance
and class composition across all six datasets, highlighting the severe class
imbalance present in the toxicity benchmarks.

\subsubsection*{ADME Regression Tasks}

\paragraph{Caco-2 Permeability (Caco2\_Wang).}
The Caco-2 cell monolayer assay is the gold-standard \emph{in vitro} surrogate for
human intestinal absorption~\cite{artursson1991caco2}.  High apparent permeability
($P_{app} > 10^{-6}$ cm/s) is associated with good oral bioavailability.  The
dataset, curated by Wang et al.~\cite{wang2016admet}, contains \textbf{910 compounds}
with target values already expressed in log(cm/s) units; no further log transformation
is applied.  Caco-2 is considered the most tractable regression task in our benchmark
because permeability is governed primarily by molecular size, lipophilicity, and
hydrogen-bond capacity---features that GNNs can capture directly from atom graphs.

\paragraph{Half-Life Prediction (Half\_Life\_Obach).}
Plasma half-life ($t_{1/2}$, hours) determines dosing frequency and is essential for
designing drugs with adequate therapeutic windows.  However, half-life is a composite
pharmacokinetic parameter influenced not only by molecular structure but also by
volume of distribution, protein binding fraction, and individual patient
variability~\cite{obach1999prediction}.  The dataset is drawn from the Obach
FDA-approved drug collection and contains \textbf{667 compounds}
spanning a wide dynamic range (0.5--100$+$ hours).  We apply a log transformation
before standardisation to reduce target skewness.

\paragraph{Hepatocyte Clearance (Clearance\_Hepatocyte\_AZ).}
Intrinsic clearance measured in freshly isolated human hepatocytes
($\mu$L/min/10$^6$ cells) captures both phase-I and phase-II metabolic routes and
is the most physiologically relevant \emph{in vitro} metabolic stability
assay~\cite{riley2001pharma}.  The dataset was generated internally at AstraZeneca
and is distributed through TDC~\cite{huang2021therapeuticsdatacommonsmachine},
comprising \textbf{1\,213 proprietary compounds}.
Low clearance values indicate slow hepatic elimination and a longer \emph{in vivo}
half-life.  This task proved the most challenging in our benchmark, with GNN models
yielding negative R$^2$ values, indicating that molecular structure alone is
insufficient to explain the variance in hepatic metabolism (see
Section~\ref{results}).

\paragraph{Microsomal Clearance (Clearance\_Microsome\_AZ).}
Human liver microsomal assays ($\mu$L/min/mg protein) measure primarily cytochrome
P450-mediated (CYP) oxidative metabolism; they offer higher throughput than
hepatocyte assays but capture only phase-I reactions~\cite{obach2006trend}.  The
AstraZeneca dataset contains \textbf{1\,102 compounds}.
Like hepatocyte clearance, microsomal clearance values are log-transformed and
then standardised using train-set statistics.

\subsubsection*{Toxicity Classification Tasks}

\paragraph{Tox21 Nuclear Receptor -- Androgen Receptor (Tox21 NR-AR).}
The Tox21 initiative~\cite{tox21data} assembled a large multi-assay toxicology
panel spanning nuclear receptor signalling and stress response pathways to enable
high-throughput environmental hazard screening.  We use the \textbf{NR-AR} assay
(androgen receptor agonism), which identifies compounds that can disrupt androgen
signalling, a key mechanism in endocrine disruption and reproductive
toxicity.  After removing molecules with invalid SMILES, the dataset contains
\textbf{7\,258 compounds}, of which only $\approx3.5\%$ are active (positive) samples
(see Figure~\ref{fig:class-distribution})---making this the most class-imbalanced
task in our benchmark.  To mitigate the imbalance, we apply positive-class
weighting in the binary cross-entropy loss, where the weight is set to the ratio of
negative to positive samples in the training fold.

\paragraph{hERG Cardiotoxicity (hERG).}
Blockade of the hERG potassium ion channel is the predominant mechanism of
drug-induced QT interval prolongation and potentially fatal cardiac arrhythmias,
and the hERG assay is a mandatory safety screen in preclinical drug
development~\cite{sanguinetti2006herg}.  The TDC hERG dataset contains
\textbf{655 compounds} with binary blocker / non-blocker labels; approximately
31\% of compounds are blockers, making this a considerably more balanced
classification task than Tox21 NR-AR (Figure~\ref{fig:class-distribution}).

\paragraph{Splitting and evaluation protocol.}
We use the TDC 2-way scaffold split function, which partitions compounds by
Bemis--Murcko scaffolds~\cite{doi:10.1021/jm9602928} so that molecules with
structurally distinct core rings appear in different folds.  This mirrors realistic
drug-discovery campaigns, where models must generalise to novel chemical series.  The
TDC scaffold split provides train and test sets; we further subdivide the train set
90/10 (train/validation) using a fixed random seed (42) to enable early stopping and
model selection without touching the held-out test set.  The final split proportions
are approximately \textbf{80 / 10 / 10} (train / val / test) across all datasets.

During hyperparameter optimisation (HPO), the test split remains \emph{fully held
out}; model selection is performed solely on the validation metric.  Final performance
is reported once on the test split after HPO is complete, preventing any implicit
information leakage from repeated test-set evaluations.

\paragraph{Target transformations.}
For the three skewed regression tasks (Half-Life, Hepatocyte and Microsomal
Clearance) we apply $y' = \log(y + \epsilon)$, where $\epsilon = 10^{-3}$ is a
small offset to handle near-zero values, followed by z-score normalisation using
training-set statistics.  Caco-2 targets are already in log(cm/s) units, so only
z-score normalisation is applied.  All normalisation parameters are computed
exclusively on the training fold and reused for validation and test sets to prevent
data leakage.  Classification targets are kept as binary integers (0/1) without
transformation.

\subsection{Optimization Algorithms}
\subsubsection{Genetic Algorithm}
\textbf{Genetic Algorithms} (GAs) draw inspiration from natural selection and evolutionary principles, advancing a population of candidate solutions through multiple generations \cite{gaopt}. Each candidate encodes a potential hyperparameter configuration, typically represented as a chromosome or encoded string. The population evolves through biologically-inspired operations—selection, crossover, and mutation—that progressively enhance fitness as measured by model performance metrics. For hyperparameter tuning, GAs excel at navigating complex, high-dimensional search spaces by effectively balancing exploration of new regions with exploitation of promising areas. The mutation and recombination mechanisms prove particularly valuable for escaping local optima, making GAs well-suited for non-convex and discrete hyperparameter landscapes.
\subsubsection{Particle Swarm Optimization}
\textbf{Particle Swarm Optimization} abstracts the collective behavior of bird flocks and schooling fish to solve continuous optimization problems. Within a swarm, each particle represents a candidate hyperparameter configuration and iteratively updates its position by considering both its own historical best performance and the best solution discovered by the entire swarm \cite{psoopt}. This combination of individual and collective learning creates an effective balance between exploratory and exploitative search, allowing PSO to converge efficiently toward high-quality hyperparameter sets. PSO is particularly effective for continuous and real-valued hyperparameters, offering the dual advantages of straightforward implementation and minimal hyperparameter tuning requirements for the optimizer itself.
\subsubsection{Artificial Bee Colony}
\textbf{Artificial Bee Colony} (ABC) optimization mimics the sophisticated foraging behavior observed in honeybee colonies \cite{abcopt}. The algorithm maintains a colony of three bee types—employed bees, onlookers, and scouts—each contributing distinct exploration and exploitation capabilities. Within this framework, candidate solutions are treated as nectar sources, and bees collaboratively search nearby solutions, assess their quality, and disseminate information throughout the colony. The ABC algorithm dynamically equilibrates exploration and exploitation, achieving robust performance across both discrete and continuous hyperparameter spaces. Its competitive results compared to other swarm-based metaheuristics demonstrate particular strength in tackling complex, multimodal optimization landscapes.
\subsubsection{Hill Climbing}
\textbf{Hill Climbing} constitutes one of the most elementary optimization heuristics, yet provides a valuable baseline reference for hyperparameter optimization tasks. The algorithm begins with an initial hyperparameter configuration and only transitions to a neighboring configuration when it demonstrates improved model performance \cite{hcopt}. This greedy approach enables fast convergence in smooth, unimodal search landscapes. Nevertheless, the complete absence of local-optima escape mechanisms renders hill climbing vulnerable to premature convergence, particularly in the non-convex hyperparameter spaces common in contemporary deep learning applications. Despite this vulnerability, hill climbing remains practical when the hyperparameter space is limited in dimensionality or when adequate initialization strategies are available.
\subsubsection{Random Search}
\textbf{Random search} stands out as an effective baseline approach for hyperparameter optimization \cite{bergstra2012random}, operating by uniformly sampling hyperparameter configurations across the search space. This method proves particularly valuable in high-dimensional optimization problems where only a limited subset of hyperparameters meaningfully influence performance outcomes. Research has shown that random search frequently surpasses grid search when the search space contains non-critical hyperparameters, as it allocates computational resources more effectively by exploring diverse configurations instead of exhaustively examining irrelevant parameter combinations. The approach offers several practical advantages: it is conceptually straightforward, readily parallelizable, and requires no prior assumptions about the underlying search space structure. These properties have established random search as a standard first-line methodology, commonly deployed before transitioning to more sophisticated optimization algorithms.
\subsubsection{Simulated Annealing}
\textbf{Simulated Annealing} (SA) adapts the physical annealing process used in metallurgy, heating and gradual cooling to produce stable crystal structures, to optimization problems. The algorithm systematically explores the solution space while accepting both performance improvements and strategic downhill moves, a feature that helps circumvent entrapment in local optima. When applied to hyperparameter optimization, SA initiates with an initial configuration and iteratively generates neighboring candidates through controlled perturbations \cite{saopt}. Acceptance decisions incorporate both performance gains and a temperature-dependent probability that permits uphill moves early in execution. As temperature gradually declines, the algorithm transitions toward a more conservative mode, concentrating optimization efforts around the best configurations identified throughout the search.

\subsubsection{Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (Optuna)}
Hyperparameter optimization is performed using \textbf{Optuna} with the Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) \cite{akiba2019optuna} as the selected sampling strategy. TPE is a Bayesian optimization algorithm that models the distributions of high-performing and low-performing hyperparameter configurations separately. By leveraging these probabilistic models, it proposes new candidate configurations that are more likely to yield improved objective values, enabling a focused and efficient exploration of the search space.
Optuna's define-by-run paradigm allows for the dynamic and conditional construction of search spaces, offering substantial flexibility when defining complex hyperparameter relationships. In addition, its built-in pruning mechanisms evaluate intermediate results during training and terminate underperforming trials early, thereby reducing computational overhead. Combined with TPE, these features provide an adaptive and resource-efficient approach to hyperparameter optimization.

\subsection{Foundation Model Baselines}
\label{sec:foundation_baselines}

We evaluate several representation-learning baselines to contextualize the optimized GNN performance: (i)~Morgan fingerprints (ECFP) with an MLP predictor, (ii)~ChemBERTa as a SMILES transformer encoder, and (iii)~MolCLR-style contrastive graph embeddings. We consider two settings for pretrained encoders: \textbf{frozen encoder + trainable MLP head} and \textbf{limited fine-tuning} (ChemBERTa-FT) where we fine-tune the encoder with a small learning-rate sweep while keeping the downstream head fixed.

For the frozen setting, embeddings are extracted once per molecule and an MLP is trained on the train split, with early stopping based on the validation split. For fairness under compute constraints, baselines use a fixed head architecture and a small learning-rate sweep, while the GNN receives the full 50-trial HPO budget. All baselines use the same dataset splits and evaluation metrics as the GNN experiments.

\subsection{Benchmarking Framework Overview}
To ensure reproducibility and fair comparison across all experiments, we developed a modular benchmarking framework that enforces consistent evaluation protocols for both GNN-based and foundation model-based pipelines. The framework coordinates dataset loading, scaffold-split partitioning, HPO execution, model training, metric computation, and statistical analysis within a unified pipeline, eliminating ad-hoc methodological inconsistencies that can confound cross-study comparisons in molecular ML~\cite{wu2018moleculenet}. Its modular design (Figure~\ref{fig:benchmark-framework}) separates experiment orchestration, model execution, and evaluation into independent components, allowing new algorithms, architectures, or datasets to be integrated without modifying the core evaluation logic.

\subsubsection{Evaluation Protocol}
The framework enforces several methodological safeguards to prevent information leakage and ensure result validity. First, all hyperparameter selection is performed exclusively on the validation split; the test set is evaluated only once after HPO completes. Second, target transformations (log-scaling, z-score normalisation) are fitted on the training fold alone and applied identically to validation and test data. Third, multi-seed retraining and paired statistical tests (Wilcoxon signed-rank) are applied uniformly across all algorithm comparisons to quantify robustness and significance.

\subsubsection{GNN and Foundation Model Pipelines}

The framework supports two distinct model pipelines. For \textbf{GNN-based models}, the pipeline handles molecular graph construction from SMILES (via RDKit), GNN forward pass, and HPO over architectural hyperparameters (layer depth, hidden dimensions, MLP head configuration, learning rate, weight decay). While the framework supports multiple GNN architectures (GCN, GAT, GIN, GraphSAGE), in this study we use GCN (GraphConv) as the backbone for all HPO experiments (see Section~\ref{sec:model_arch} for architecture justification).

For \textbf{foundation model baselines}, the pipeline extracts molecular embeddings from pretrained encoders (ChemBERTa, MolCLR) or fingerprint generators (Morgan-FP, MolE-FP), then trains a downstream MLP predictor on the frozen representations. This frozen-encoder protocol isolates the quality of pretrained representations from downstream optimization effects, providing a controlled comparison against end-to-end GNN training.

All training runs are logged with per-trial hyperparameter configurations, validation trajectories, and final test metrics, enabling post-hoc analysis of HPO convergence and search space coverage.

\subsubsection{Model Architecture and Training}
\label{sec:model_arch}

\paragraph{Model Architecture.}
We use a graph neural network backbone based on stacked Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) layers~\cite{kipf2017gcn} with batch normalization and ReLU activation. We selected GCN over more expressive alternatives (GAT, GIN, GraphSAGE) for several reasons: (i)~GCN provides a well-established, widely reproduced baseline in molecular benchmarks~\cite{wu2018moleculenet}, ensuring comparability with prior work; (ii)~its lower parameter count and simpler attention-free aggregation reduce overfitting risk on our small datasets (655--7{,}258 molecules); and (iii)~our focus is on HPO strategy comparison rather than architecture search, so a stable backbone minimizes confounding architectural effects. Each molecule is represented as a molecular graph constructed from SMILES using RDKit. Atom features include atomic number, degree, formal charge, hybridization, aromaticity, ring membership, hydrogen count, and atomic mass (8 features). The GNN encoder produces node embeddings which are aggregated into a graph-level representation using concatenated global mean pooling and global max pooling. The pooled representation is passed to an MLP prediction head with dropout and batch normalization.


\textbf{Training Configuration.} All models were trained using the Adam optimizer with default parameters and a batch size of 32. During HPO, models were trained for a maximum of 50 epochs per trial with early stopping (patience~=~12). Regression models were optimized using mean squared error (MSE) loss, while classification models used binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss with logits and incorporated positive class weighting to address class imbalance. Target values were standardized to have zero mean and unit variance. Evaluation was performed with a batch size of 64, and gradient clipping was applied with a maximum norm of 1.0.

\paragraph{Algorithm Performance Ranking}

Across the four ADME regression datasets, no single algorithm consistently dominated all tasks, suggesting that the optimal choice of HPO strategy is problem-dependent. PSO, ABC, and GA achieved the best performance on Half\_Life\_Obach (RMSE = 21.66) while Random Search excelled on Caco2\_Wang (RMSE = 0.0027), Clearance\_Hepatocyte\_AZ (RMSE = 68.22), and Clearance\_Microsome\_AZ (RMSE = 38.75). \noindent\textbf{Key insight: optimization behavior under scaffold split.}
A central observation from our experiments is that scaffold-based evaluation changes the relative effectiveness of optimization strategies compared to random split settings commonly reported in prior work. Under scaffold splits,
the validation landscape becomes noisier and less smooth due to structural distribution shift, reducing the advantage of adaptive metaheuristic strategies. As a result, simpler approaches such as Random Search remain highly competitive,
while population-based methods require larger budgets to consistently outperform. This suggests that previously reported optimizer superiority may partially arise
from evaluation protocol choices rather than algorithmic advantages.

\paragraph{Convergence Characteristics}

Analysis of the optimization trajectories (Figure~\ref{fig:convergence}) reveals distinct behavioral patterns among the algorithms. PSO and ABC demonstrated rapid initial convergence, typically identifying near-optimal configurations within the first 10–15 trials out of 50 total. This aggressive exploration strategy proved particularly effective for smooth optimization landscapes like Caco2 permeability. In contrast, SA exhibited more gradual convergence with consistent improvement across all trials, making it more robust for challenging tasks with many local optima, such as the clearance datasets.

GA and HC showed inconsistent performance, occasionally achieving competitive results but often converging prematurely to suboptimal configurations. GA's underperformance may be attributed to the limited population size (16 individuals) and the fixed 50-trial budget, which may still be insufficient for effective evolutionary search in a high-dimensional hyperparameter space.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Our systematic evaluation of seven HPO strategies across six ADMET tasks yields several insights with practical implications for computational drug discovery.

\subsection{No Universal Optimizer Exists}

The most striking finding is the task-dependence of HPO algorithm rankings. Random Search won on three of four regression tasks among the NiaPy-based algorithms, PSO achieved the best result on Half-Life, and metaheuristics (SA, ABC) dominated the classification tasks. TPE (Optuna) achieved the best standalone result on Clearance\_Hepatocyte\_AZ, though its expanded search space (including dropout) limits direct comparison. This variability suggests that the optimal HPO strategy depends on the interplay between dataset characteristics (size, noise level, class balance) and the structure of the hyperparameter landscape.

Importantly, no metaheuristic algorithm achieved a statistically significant improvement over Random Search (Wilcoxon signed-rank, $p > 0.05$) under a 50-trial budget with scaffold-split evaluation. This challenges the assumption that sophisticated optimization is always necessary and aligns with the findings of Bergstra and Bengio~\cite{bergstra2012random}, who showed that random search is competitive when only a subset of hyperparameters are influential. For practitioners with limited compute budgets, Random Search remains a strong default choice, while population-based methods like PSO and SA offer more consistent cross-task performance.

\subsection{Scaffold Split Alters Optimizer Rankings}

A central methodological observation is that scaffold-based evaluation changes the relative effectiveness of optimization strategies compared to random-split settings commonly reported in prior work. Under scaffold splits, the validation landscape becomes noisier due to structural distribution shift between train and validation folds, reducing the advantage of adaptive metaheuristics that exploit landscape smoothness. This suggests that previously reported optimizer superiority may partially arise from evaluation protocol choices rather than intrinsic algorithmic advantages. Our findings reinforce the importance of scaffold-split evaluation~\cite{wu2018moleculenet, yang2019analyzing} as the standard for molecular benchmarking, and caution against generalizing HPO recommendations derived from random-split experiments.

\subsection{Dataset Difficulty and Structure-Only Limitations}

The benchmarked tasks exhibited vastly different levels of predictability from molecular structure alone. Caco-2 permeability achieved moderate performance (R$^2$ = 0.482), consistent with the well-established relationship between membrane transport and physicochemical descriptors such as lipophilicity and molecular size. In contrast, half-life prediction (R$^2$ = 0.004) and hepatocyte clearance (R$^2$ = $-$1.019) proved essentially unpredictable, highlighting fundamental limitations of structure-only models for complex pharmacokinetic processes influenced by enzyme kinetics, transporter activity, protein binding, and individual patient variability~\cite{obach1999prediction}. These results suggest that for such endpoints, molecular structure alone provides insufficient information, and future approaches should consider integrating protein target context, physiological parameters, or multi-task learning across related endpoints.

Both toxicity endpoints achieved strong discriminative performance (hERG AUC = 0.825, Tox21 AUC = 0.742), indicating that GNNs can effectively learn structural alerts for safety liabilities. The high F1 score on hERG (0.809) demonstrates practical utility for virtual screening to deprioritize potentially cardiotoxic compounds early in drug development.

\subsection{Task-Specific GNNs versus Foundation Models}

Our comparison reveals that optimized task-specific GNNs can match or exceed frozen pretrained foundation models on most tasks, particularly on toxicity classification where graph-level structural patterns are strong predictors. On hERG, the GNN (AUC = 0.825) substantially outperformed ChemBERTa (0.770) and Morgan-FP (0.611), suggesting that learned graph representations capture hERG-relevant pharmacophoric features more effectively than SMILES-based or fingerprint-based encodings.

The notable exception is Clearance\_Hepatocyte\_AZ, where all foundation model baselines outperform the GNN. Although none of the models achieve meaningful R$^2$ on this task, pretrained representations provide a more stable starting point, likely because the regularisation implicit in large-scale pretraining prevents the severe overfitting that plagues end-to-end training on a difficult 1{,}213-compound dataset. This result suggests that for particularly challenging metabolic endpoints, frozen pretrained encoders can serve as a useful regulariser, and a hybrid strategy combining pretrained initialisation with task-specific fine-tuning may yield the best of both approaches.

\subsection{Limitations}

\textbf{Limited HPO budget.} Our study uses a 50-trial budget per algorithm. While this reflects practical compute constraints, larger budgets may further improve population-based methods (GA, PSO, ABC) that require more evaluations for effective evolutionary search. Future work should evaluate multi-seed HPO runs to disentangle optimizer variance from model variance.

\textbf{Frozen foundation models.} Our comparison used pretrained encoders as frozen feature extractors rather than fine-tuning their weights end-to-end, which likely underestimates their potential. Even as a frozen encoder, ChemBERTa showed moderate scaffold-split degradation on Tox21 (Val AUC = 0.896, Test AUC = 0.73), while MolCLR degraded to near-random performance (Test AUC = 0.54), suggesting fundamental limitations of contrastive graph pretraining for this specific task.

\textbf{Class imbalance.} The Tox21 dataset suffered from extreme class imbalance (3.5\% positive rate), resulting in poor minority class recall despite high accuracy. Future studies should explore graph-aware resampling techniques, focal loss, and cost-sensitive learning to improve sensitivity on rare toxic compounds.

\textbf{Molecular featurization.} This study used standard 2D atom-level features (atomic number, degree, formal charge, hybridization, aromaticity, ring membership, hydrogen count, atomic mass). Incorporating 3D conformer information, learned atomic embeddings, or quantum chemical descriptors may improve performance on physicochemical tasks like permeability and clearance where spatial molecular geometry plays a critical role.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented a reproducible benchmarking framework for systematic evaluation of hyperparameter optimization strategies for Graph Neural Networks on molecular ADMET prediction. Through 2{,}100+ training runs across seven HPO algorithms and six TDC datasets under scaffold-split evaluation, we demonstrated that no single optimizer universally dominates: Random Search is competitive on regression tasks, while metaheuristics (SA, ABC) excel on classification. Scaffold-split evaluation substantially alters optimizer rankings compared to random-split settings, cautioning against generalizing HPO recommendations across evaluation protocols. Optimized task-specific GNNs match or exceed frozen foundation model baselines on most tasks, though pretrained representations provide more stable predictions on the most challenging metabolic endpoint. Multi-seed validation and statistical significance testing confirm the robustness of these findings. Our framework and results provide practical guidance for HPO strategy selection in molecular property prediction under realistic evaluation conditions.
